01Vision and guiding principles

By 2031, every AI device in Indian care: proven, tracked, answerable

“
Our 5-year vision (2031)

By 2031, India will set the benchmark for trustworthy AI in healthcare: devices validated on India's diverse population, open to public scrutiny through a national registry, and approved under rules that peer regulators choose to rely on.

2031TARGET

Problems solved

Bring proven AI screening for imaging, ECG, retinal and pathology to districts where specialists are scarce.

Risks better managed

Catch bias, drift and unsafe updates early, and make every device secure against cyber attack and data misuse by design.

Who benefits

Safer care for patients, AI tools that clinicians can understand and question, and a clear, predictable approval path for innovators.

Trust at home and abroad

Publish the safety evidence, so patients trust the device at the bedside and peer regulators accept India's approvals.

Aligned with MeitY India AI Governance Guidelines (Nov 2025, seven sutras), ICMR Ethical Guidelines for AI in Biomedical Research and Healthcare (2023) and CDSCO Medical Device Software Guidance (Jul 2026).

03Priority risks and mitigation

AI Risk Categories: Brainstorming Potential AI Risks

IdentificationWhich risks, and past cases?
ImpactWho and what is hurt most?
Governance gapDo laws and regulators cover it?
MitigationShort-term now, long-term later

Technical

Bias, explainability, reliability, cyber

Bias, opaque outputs, drift and cyber flaws. Cases: a sepsis model missed 2 in 3 cases when tested independently (JAMA IM 2021); pulse oximeters hid low oxygen ~3× more often in Black patients (NEJM 2020).
Missed or wrong diagnoses at screening scale, worst for groups missing from training data. Hospital ransomware (AIIMS 2022) is a national-security threat.
PARTLY CDSCO guidance names these risks but is advisory; change protocol is optional; no device cyber standard is named.
Now: Indian validation for Class C and D; mandatory change protocol; named cyber standards.
Later: BODH fairness testing; real-world monitoring; CERT-In device testing.

Social & ethical

Exclusion, misinformation, trust, jobs

Exclusion of under-served patients, misinformation from generative AI, loss of trust, changing jobs. Cases: retinal AI in Thai clinics struggled with poor-quality images (CHI 2020); dermatology AI did worse on dark skin (Sci Adv 2022).
Rural, tribal, low-literacy and older patients lose out first; imaging and lab staff need reskilling; trust in digital health falls.
GAP No duty to tell patients that AI was used; no patient AI-literacy programme.
Now: Disclose AI use in local languages; human sign-off for diagnosis.
Later: NMC curricula; staff reskilling; community monitors on audits.

Legal & governance

Data protection, accountability, split mandates

Weak data protection, accountability gaps, fragmented mandates. Case: when AI errs, there is no clear answer to who is liable: developer, hospital or clinician.
Patients cannot get redress; privacy and consent rights are at risk; innovators face legal uncertainty.
PARTLY DPDP core duties start May 2027; no AI-specific liability; mandates split across CDSCO, DPB, CERT-In, ICMR and NHA.
Now: Oversight Cell to coordinate; patient grievance channel; DPDP-ready consent.
Later: Liability guidance under CPA 2019 and MDR; registry mandate.

Environmental

Energy, carbon, e-waste

High energy use for training and running models, carbon footprint, and e-waste from retired devices and servers.
Higher energy costs for hospitals and data centres; more e-waste; a growing climate cost as AI scales.
GAP E-waste rules exist, but the energy use of AI devices is not tracked or reported.
Now: Voluntary reporting of compute and energy use in the registry.
Later: Green procurement criteria; take-back under e-waste rules.
ARE ENFORCEMENT BODIES EQUIPPED? Not yet. CDSCO has no dedicated AI-device unit; AI audit skills among reviewers, test labs and hospitals are not benchmarked; device cyber-testing is thin. Fix: Oversight Cell, certified reviewer training and accredited labs in Years 1-2.
BALANCING RISK AND INNOVATION
Proportionate: light for Class A and B, strict for C and D. Sandbox: test early on real Indian data, under supervision. Pre-agreed updates: improve models without re-approval, within limits.

04Governance model

Our model: Hybrid, risk classes plus rights safeguards

Risk-based

Regulates by risk level (EU AI Act style)

Rights-based

Regulates through fundamental rights (UNESCO style)

CHOSEN MODEL: HYBRIDrisk classes as the base, rights safeguards on top

Closer to the risk end: India already classifies devices by risk, so we build on that law and add rights safeguards where risk class alone falls short.

Why hybrid is the right fit for India
1

The risk base already exists

MDR 2017 sorts devices into Classes A to D. CDSCO's Jul 2026 software guidance classifies software by how much its output drives decisions and how serious the condition is.

MDR 2017 · CDSCO MDSW
2

AI risks need firmer duties

CDSCO names bias, lack of explainability, drift, poor generalisability and hallucination, but the guidance is advisory and change protocols depend on each product's risk.

CDSCO MDSW, 21 Jul 2026
3

Risk class misses rights

ICMR's 10 patient-centric principles (autonomy, privacy, accountability, fairness) and DPDP data rights, with core duties from May 2027, need their own safeguards.

ICMR 2023 · DPDP Rules 2025
4

Fits India's AI policy

MeitY's guidelines propose no standalone AI law: sector regulators lead and an AI Safety Institute supports testing. So CDSCO leads, not a new regulator.

MeitY, 5 Nov 2025
5

Speaks the global language

IMDRF good machine learning practice, FDA change-control plans and the EU AI Act all pair risk-based oversight with lifecycle controls. CDSCO joined IMDRF as an affiliate in 2024.

IMDRF N88 · FDA · EU

04Governance model

Trade-offs we accept, and how we stay aligned with the world

Identified trade-offs, and how we manage them
Innovation ⇄ Regulation
Stricter evidence slows launches but prevents harm.
How we manage it: Proportionate duties: lighter for Class A and B, strict for C and D; sandbox for early access.
National ⇄ Global
India-specific validation costs more than relying on FDA or EU clearance alone.
How we manage it: Accept foreign evidence and add targeted Indian validation, as CDSCO guidance allows; shared BODH benchmarks cut cost.
Data access ⇄ Privacy
AI needs health records; DPDP core duties begin May 2027.
How we manage it: Consented, de-identified data through ABDM, with ethics committee review.
Feasibility ⇄ Ambition
CDSCO needs staff and AI skills before it can enforce.
How we manage it: Phase in: Oversight Cell and training first, binding standards in Year 3.
Flexibility ⇄ Certainty
An optional change protocol is flexible but gives weak certainty.
How we manage it: Mandatory change protocol for adaptive Class C and D AI, with pre-agreed limits.

Regulatory fees for audits and registry listing; cost-sharing for the sandbox; a permanent budget line.

F. Reflection: assessing our readiness
Urgent gaps (Years 1-2): No AI-device unit in CDSCO; uncertified reviewers; no named device cyber standard.
Needs legislative reform: MDR amendment for binding change protocol and validation; registry mandate; liability guidance under CPA 2019.
Best built through partners: Training (ICMR, IITs), compute and testing (IndiaAI, Safety Institute), benchmarks (BODH, AIKosh), standards (IMDRF, WHO).
Sustainable after Year 5: Fees for audits and registry listing plus a permanent budget line, so oversight does not depend on one-time grants.

First proof, then treatment

No AI medical device reaches an Indian patient without a public record.

One open national register of every licensed AI medical device: how it performs on Indian patients, who is accountable for it, and every change made to its algorithm.

Indian-population evidence is published for every device before it reaches patients.
